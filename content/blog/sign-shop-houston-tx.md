Searching "sign shop Houston" brings up print counters, national resellers, and full-service fabricators all at once. They can look alike online, but they are very different businesses. This guide covers how to tell them apart and what to ask before you sign anything.

## What does a full-service sign shop actually do?

A permanent business sign, such as channel letters, a monument sign, or a lit cabinet, involves five jobs. Someone has to design it and measure the site, get it permitted, fabricate it, wire it, and install it. A full-service sign shop handles all five, so one company is accountable from the first sketch to the final inspection.

Other businesses handle only part of that chain. A print shop is great for banners and decals but usually doesn't build or permit structural signs. A reseller takes your order and passes the build to someone else, which adds a middle layer when something needs fixing. Neither is wrong for the right job. For a permanent exterior sign, though, you want to know exactly who is building it and who stands behind it.

The simplest test is to ask where your sign will actually be built and who will install it. A clear answer is a good sign. A vague one is worth a follow-up question.

## Five questions to ask any Houston sign shop

### 1. Are you a City of Houston licensed sign contractor?

Inside Houston city limits, exterior sign work for others must be done by a City-licensed sign contractor, and that contractor is the one who pulls the sign permit. If a shop asks you to pull the permit yourself, that usually means it can't.

### 2. Who handles the electrical permit for a lit sign?

Illuminated signs need a separate electrical permit, and the electrical work must be done by a TDLR-licensed Master Sign Electrician or Master Electrician. Ask who that is on your job and whether the electrical inspection is part of the price.

### 3. Will you check my landlord's rules and my jurisdiction first?

Your sign has to satisfy two sets of rules. First is the government, which could be the City of Houston, another city, or Harris County if you're outside city limits. Second is your landlord's sign criteria, which can limit letter size, color, and lighting even when the city would allow more. A careful shop checks both before designing. Our [permit guide](/blog/houston-business-sign-permits) explains which office issues permits where, and our [sign permits page](/sign-permits) covers the suburbs.

### 4. Does my sign need engineering?

The City of Houston requires sealed engineered drawings for larger signs and signs mounted higher above grade, designed to the local construction code and wind-load standards. A shop that has done this work will know right away whether your sign crosses that line, and will include the engineering in the estimate.

### 5. What exactly is in the estimate, and when do I get it?

A trustworthy estimate separates design, permits, electrical, fabrication, and installation, so you can see where the money goes. It should come after someone has seen your site. Photos can get a conversation started, but a firm number needs real measurements, a look at the mounting surface, and a check for power.

## Red flags when hiring a sign shop

- **A firm price before anyone sees the site.** Wall construction, electrical access, and landlord rules all change the job, so an instant quote often grows once the real work starts.
- **"You can pull the permit yourself."** For most permanent signs in Houston, a licensed sign contractor has to pull it.
- **No mockup.** You should see the sign on a photo of your building before it's fabricated, not after.
- **Signs placed in the public right-of-way.** Private signs on sidewalks, medians, and road right-of-way are unlawful under Texas law, and cities remove them. A shop that suggests it is taking a shortcut that ends up costing you.
- **Silence about the operating permit.** Inside Houston, a permitted sign gets an operating decal, and that operating permit renews every year. A good shop tells you this up front so the renewal doesn't catch you off guard.

## What drives the cost of a sign

Price depends on sign type and size, lighting, whether engineering is required, electrical work, permit and plan-review fees, and installation conditions such as access, height, and foundations. Special districts add their own reviews. Historic districts, for example, need a Certificate of Appropriateness before the city issues a sign permit.
