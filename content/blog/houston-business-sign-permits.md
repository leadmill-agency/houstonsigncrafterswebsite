A sign that's the wrong size, too bright, or installed without approval is one of the most expensive mistakes a business owner can make. This guide walks through what actually needs a permit, what the city looks at, and how to get approved the first time.

## Which signs need a permit in Houston?

As a general rule, **permanent signs attached to a building or mounted on the ground need a permit**. This includes channel letters, monument signs, pylon signs, and most illuminated signage. Temporary signs like banners may have separate, time-limited rules. Small window graphics and some interior signs often don't require a permit, but it's worth confirming before you order.

The safest approach: assume a permanent exterior sign needs a permit until proven otherwise, and verify before fabrication, not after.

## What does the city check?

When you apply for a sign permit, the review typically looks at:

- **Size and height** relative to your building frontage and the road
- **Illumination**: whether and how the sign is lit
- **Placement and setback** from property lines and right-of-way
- **Structural and electrical safety**, especially for larger or lit signs

On top of city rules, **your landlord's sign criteria** matter just as much. Many Houston retail centers and office parks have their own standards for letter style, color, and mounting. A sign can be city-legal and still get rejected by a landlord, so both need to line up before you build.

## Who issues the permit depends on where the sign sits

"Houston" covers three different permitting worlds:

- **Inside the City of Houston**, the Sign Code (Chapter 46 of the city building code) controls exterior signs visible from streets and sidewalks. Houston Public Works' Sign Administration issues the permit and attaches an **operating permit tag** to the approved sign — and that tag renews annually. The city also no longer issues most new off-premise (billboard-style) sign permits.
- **In unincorporated Harris County** — including the county's extra-territorial jurisdiction, scenic corridors, and toll-road frontage — the county Engineering Department issues sign permits through its E-Permits system. Submittals typically include a site plan of all signs, a survey for new ground signs, and engineered plans for taller structures.
- **Along interstates and state highways**, TxDOT regulates off-premises advertising and requires a state license plus a location-specific permit. TxDOT doesn't permit on-premises business signs, but for sites in a big city's ETJ it will ask for a copy of the municipal permit.

## What usually doesn't need a new permit

Changing the advertising copy or ornamental features of an already-permitted sign generally doesn't require a new sign permit. But the exceptions matter: converting a static sign to an electronic or changeable-message face triggers a new review, anything involving electrical or structural changes needs its own permits, and if a sign is removed or destroyed, the replacement must meet **current** code — not the code it was originally approved under.

## Rules that catch business owners off guard

- **Signs in the public right-of-way are unlawful** under state law and city ordinance — a sidewalk or median is never fair game.
- **Banners, inflatables, and pennants** aren't eligible for a city sign permit as permanent signage, and the city can impound them.
- **Projecting signs that extend over the right-of-way** need a separate encroachment permit from the Office of the City Engineer.
- **Historic districts, scenic corridors, and special districts** layer extra design and placement rules on top of the base Sign Code.
- **Licensing is real**: exterior sign work in Houston must be performed by a city-licensed sign contractor, and illuminated signs need a separate electrical permit pulled by a licensed sign electrician. Larger wall signs — for example above 8 feet or over 60 square feet — also need engineered drawings.

Houston's famous lack of zoning trips people up here. No zoning does **not** mean no sign rules: permits, operating tags, and inspections apply to most exterior business signs, and being on private property doesn't exempt you from setbacks or district restrictions.

## How to avoid a costly redo

The business owners who get tripped up usually order a sign first and deal with approvals later. Flip that order:

1. **Confirm your lease sign criteria** before choosing a design.
2. **Check city size, height, and illumination limits** for your address.
3. **Get a permit-ready drawing** that documents dimensions and mounting.
4. **Submit and get approval** before fabrication starts.
